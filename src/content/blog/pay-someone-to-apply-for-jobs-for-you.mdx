## Key takeaways

- **It is allowed and common to delegate the repetitive part.** Finding roles inside written rules, completing standard fields and logging each submission are good tasks to hand over.
- **Price follows who does the work.** Software costs least, a dedicated person costs more, and a reverse recruiter, who adds outreach and coaching, costs most.
- **You keep the decisions that carry weight.** Work authorization, salary, relocation and anything legal or personal should stay with you.
- **Ask for proof before you pay.** A record of the job, status and resume used for every application is the minimum.

## What should you delegate, and what should you keep?

Good tasks to hand over are repetitive and governed by facts:

- Finding roles inside a written search profile.
- Checking location, seniority, salary and work arrangement against your rules.
- Completing standard employment and education fields.
- Uploading an approved resume and cover letter.
- Recording the job link, date, status and the document used.
- Flagging any question that needs your decision.

Keep direct control of facts with legal, ethical or personal weight: work authorization and sponsorship, salary expectations, relocation, disability and demographic disclosures, criminal history and conflicts of interest. Give your assistant approved answers where the truth is stable, and require it to ask rather than guess when a form differs from your approved wording.

## What do you still do yourself?

Paying for application work does not hand over your search. You still:

1. **Choose the target.** Pick the roles, locations, salary floor and dealbreakers. An unclear target produces unclear applications.
2. **Own the facts.** Your resume must stay true. Tailoring should emphasize real experience, never invent a skill, title or result.
3. **Check the first results.** Review the first batch before you let volume grow.
4. **Do the interviews.** Nobody else can do them for you.
5. **Respond to employers.** Replies come to you, and you decide what happens next.

## What red flags should you watch for?

The full list is in [are job application services legit?](/blog/are-job-application-services-legit), with ten checks you can run before paying.

- **A guaranteed job or interview.** No provider controls employer decisions. If a service sells a guaranteed outcome, read its written terms and treat the claim with care. See [what to do when a service produces no interviews](/blog/job-application-service-no-interviews).
- **A request to pay for the promise of a job itself.** Paying an assistant for application work you can see is different from paying someone for access, equipment, training or a promised position. Be careful with any offer that asks for the second kind of payment.
- **No record of what was sent.** At minimum you should keep the employer, role, link, date, status and resume version. Read [what proof an application assistant should give you](/blog/what-proof-should-job-application-assistant-provide).
- **Invented experience.** Walk away from any service that offers to add skills or jobs you do not have.
- **Broad credential sharing.** Avoid handing over a primary email or LinkedIn password when a safer workflow exists. Ask for the exact process first.
- **Automation that breaks a platform's rules.** LinkedIn's help page on automated activity says it does not allow third-party software or browser extensions that automate activity on its website, and that this violates its User Agreement ([LinkedIn Help: Automated activity on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1340567), fetched November 1, 2026). A service should explain where and how it operates. Read [application safety](/safety).
- **A percentage of your salary.** Some providers add a percentage of first-year salary on top of a monthly fee, so ask before you pay.
- **Terms that are not in writing.** Get cancellation and refund conditions, unused volume and daily limits in writing before you pay, and ask which sites are supported.

## Who does paying someone to apply suit, and who does it not suit?

It suits you when time is the bottleneck: you work full time, you are tired of repeating the same forms, and your target roles are clear. It is a weaker fit when the real problem is an unclear target role or a resume that is not working. Fix those first, or pair a service with a coach. If you want outreach to hiring managers and positioning for senior roles, a reverse recruiter does more than application work and costs more. If you want the lowest price and are happy to manage the search yourself, software is the cheaper route and you do the checking.

## Is it worth paying someone to apply?

It is worth considering when the hours you get back are worth more to you than the plan costs and your target roles are already clear. You are paying for the application work, and employers still decide who they interview and hire. For five measures and a worked example, read [is a job application service worth it?](/blog/is-a-job-application-service-worth-it)

## How do you choose a provider?

1. **Who submits.** A named person, software or a mix.
2. **Proof.** Can you see the job, the resume used and what was entered in the form?
3. **Resume honesty.** Does it adjust the wording of your real experience, or invent skills?
4. **Terms.** Monthly or prepaid, cancellation, any salary percentage, and what any "guarantee" requires in writing.
5. **Fit with your bottleneck.** If forms and time are the problem, delegate. If you cannot name a target role, get help with that first.

Start with a small audited batch and relax approval only when the results are consistent.
